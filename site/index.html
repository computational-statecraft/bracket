Free and open source · runs on one computer

A drafting table for rooms that have to [agree].

bracket puts one draft on one screen in front of a room, with one person at the keyboard. People write the text. The machine only proposes other words: stronger or weaker, shorter or longer, plainer or more formal. It is not allowed to add a claim, a name or a number, and that rule is enforced in the code, not left to a prompt.

How a room uses it

One text, one keyboard, one room.

Groups that must agree on a short text (a communiqué, a set of principles, a joint statement) spend most of their time on wording: whether to encourage or urge, whether a clause says should or will. bracket makes that work faster without taking it out of the room's hands.

1

Paste the draft

It is split into numbered sentences, keeping headings and lists. The room reads it on the projector; nobody else needs a device.

2

Argue over a word

Double-click it. Alternatives appear beneath, at the strength, length and register the facilitator dials in with the arrow keys.

3

Keep disagreement visible

Unsettled wording stays in the text in square brackets, as negotiators write it: [encourage / urge]. A sentence with an open bracket cannot be marked agreed.

4

Leave with a record

Every proposal shown, accepted or refused is logged. The session ends with the agreed text, each sentence's history, and any dissent.

The record, as it is written

    The rule

    The machine proposes words, never positions.

    Every proposal, from any model, passes a check before the room sees it. The check does not rely on the model behaving: a proposal that breaks the rule is removed, and one that might change the meaning is marked for the room to decide.

    Removed

    A proposal that names a country, organization or person not already in the sentence.

    Flagged ▲

    A changed number, a changed modal (should → must), added or dropped negation, a large jump in length.

    Quoted, never generated

    Agreed language: sentences from texts your group has already adopted, shown word for word with their source.

    Left alone

    Bracketed text. The models are told not to touch it, and the room settles it.

    Try the check

    Members will work towards a common approach to search and rescue, consistent with international law.

    A simplified copy of the app's check, running in this page. The app's version uses a language model of English grammar to find names, not capital letters.

    Why build it this way

    Every shortcut has a cost. The question is which cost is worth paying.

    There is a wide range of ways to work with advanced technology, from handing it the whole task to refusing it altogether. Most tools for reaching agreement with AI sit near the first end: the model writes the shared text and people react to it. That works for gathering the views of thousands. It sits badly with a small room of people who are accountable for every word.

    The cost of delegation is measurable. In controlled studies, a writing assistant with a point of view shifted what people wrote and, afterwards, what they said they believed, mostly without their noticing; and suggestions pull prose towards the model's own style. Negotiators already know a milder version of the problem: research on diplomats drafting in shared documents finds that features like track changes shape the negotiation itself, and can produce texts nobody quite intended.

    So bracket limits the machine to what it is good at and a room is slow at: finding the next word. Everything else stays with the people, and the software takes its working objects from negotiating practice: the single text that every party edits, square brackets for wording not yet agreed, and agreed language carried over word for word. Whether this helps a room keep control of its text is the question bracket is built to test. We do not claim that it improves outcomes.

    What is new, and what is not

    None of the parts is new. Drafting from a single shared text is a 1970s negotiating procedure; live editors, model rewording, local models and text search are all off the shelf. What bracket adds is the combination and the constraint: brackets that block agreement, quoted precedent inside a live drafting screen, a control for the strength of the wording, and a reword-only rule enforced in code, with a record of everything offered and refused.

    Download

    Download, open, choose a model.

    No terminal, nothing else to install. bracket opens in its own window and everything runs on your computer; nothing leaves it unless you switch on the optional Claude tier.

    MacApple silicon (M1 or later) · .dmg WindowsWindows 10 or 11, 64-bit · .zip Linuxx86-64, Ubuntu 22.04 or later · .tar.gz

    First open

    1. Mac: drag bracket to Applications and open it. The app is not yet signed, so macOS asks once: choose Done, then System Settings → Privacy & Security → Open Anyway.
    2. Windows: extract the zip and open bracket.exe. If SmartScreen appears, choose More info → Run anyway.
    3. Linux: extract and run bracket/bracket.

    Choosing a model

    The setup screen lists what is already on your computer first: models in Ollama, and Apple's on-device model on Macs with Apple Intelligence. Otherwise it recommends a download for your computer's memory, from 3 GB to 17 GB, fetched once and then used offline.

    Optional: Claude

    Paste an Anthropic API key on the setup screen and the facilitator can ask Claude for more proposals by pressing C. Only the sentence being worked on is sent, and only then.

    Source code, documentation and the local API: github.com/computational-statecraft/bracket.
